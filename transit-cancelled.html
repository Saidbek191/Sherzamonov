The Country of the Eternal Rahmon: Transit in Tajikistan is Suspended

On October 11, Tajikistan will hold its next presidential election: Emomali Rahmon intends to become head of state for the fifth time. In fact, he has been in power since November 1992, making him one of the world’s longest-serving political leaders (only four African politicians have been in power longer). Earlier this year, many experts speculated that Rahmon planned to formally transfer the reins of power to his son, Rustam Emomali, especially since in 2016 the presidential age requirement was lowered from 35 to 30 years — supposedly for Rustam’s benefit. However, something went wrong, and Rahmon’s son did not run in this election. According to opposition political émigré Alim Sherzamonov, the reason lies in internal contradictions within the ruling clan.

No one expects surprises in the upcoming presidential election this weekend: Emomali Rahmon has long since eliminated or exiled all real political rivals (see an overview article summarizing 25 years of his rule). Still, some unexpected events have occurred. Firstly, since 2013 — immediately after the previous election — analysts have been discussing a power transition in the country. According to the law, which had been repeatedly amended to suit Rahmon, this was supposed to be his last term as president. It seemed there was no room for new political fantasies. Of course, he could have transformed the country into a parliamentary republic and named himself prime minister, but for Rahmon’s ego, this would have been a kind of humiliation. It was easier for him to step down as president while leaving behind a successor.

This was the expectation — right up until the final day for candidate nominations. The only thing that could have energized the presidential campaign was the nomination of Rustam Emomali, Rahmon’s eldest son. In 2016, constitutional changes lowered the presidential age limit from 35 to 30, specifically for this purpose. At the same time, the Constitution removed any term limits for the “Founder of Peace and National Unity — Leader of the Nation.” This grand title was bestowed on Rahmon by parliament a few months earlier, formally preserving his right to run countless times — though in practice, it seemed unnecessary, since the “Leader of the Nation” law meant he would retain all levers of power even after passing the presidency to a successor.

What motivated Rahmon not to let his son run remains unclear. Some analysts believe that Vladimir Putin did not give his blessing to this power transition. Others suggest that Rahmon decided to run himself because he feared his generals and the “old guard,” who found young Rustam insufficiently charismatic and authoritative. In my opinion, while these theories have some validity, they are unlikely. The Kremlin has lost much of its former influence in the republic, and Putin’s opinion probably matters less to Rahmon than that of Chairman Xi Jinping. Regarding possible problems with the “old guard,” unlike most “normal” authoritarian regimes, Tajikistan practically lacks alternative centers of power — there are almost no independent state institutions or major business groups. All power and resources are literally concentrated in the hands of one family. Therefore, in my view, Rustam had nothing to fear from any generals; his real problem was that he failed to gain recognition within his own family.

The real problem for Rustam Emomali is that he has failed to gain recognition within his own family.

The key figures here are Rustam’s sisters, who harbor no unjustified jealousy. Over the past four years — since the constitutional referendum — the unsuccessful heir has built a team of so-called “young security officers” and “technocrats.” This team has no place for Rustam’s many brothers-in-law and their relatives, who play a significant role in the hierarchy of the Rahmon family’s power. For Rahmon’s daughters, their father’s hold on power is much more reliable and stable. They also have the support of Saymumin Yatimov, chairman of the State Committee for National Security (GKNB), the strongest “non-family” player. Thus, in my view, Rahmon made a political choice in favor of his daughters. It is the family that prevented Rustam from becoming president — and I believe he never will, because in seven years his younger brother will be nearly thirty, and if necessary, the age limit can be removed altogether.

Of course, the game is far from over for Rustam. He currently holds the position of speaker of the upper house of parliament and, according to the Constitution, will head the country if Rahmon is unable to perform his duties. But for now, the only possible intrigue in this “election” died before it was born.

Some liveliness was brought into the election race by a young lawyer, Faromuz Irgashev — a peer of Rustam from Khorog, the administrative center of the Badakhshan Autonomous Region. He announced on his YouTube channel his intention to exercise his constitutional right to run for president. Initially, opponents of the government on social media questioned the sincerity of this young man’s intentions, suspecting him to be a government project aimed at injecting fresh blood into the electoral process. Then the GKNB troll factory launched an attack on Irgashev, focusing especially on his recent trip to the USA. However, it soon became clear that neither group was right. In fact, Irgashev, well versed in legislative nuances, drew attention to the contradiction between the Constitution and the presidential election law — and decided to use this fact to make a name for himself. He succeeded with minimal costs, and most importantly, as a professional lawyer, he pointed out serious flaws in the presidential election law. It turned out that in Tajikistan it is practically impossible to run as an independent candidate, even though, according to the Constitution, every citizen who meets the age requirement has the right to do so. The circle of entities entitled to nominate a presidential candidate is very narrow. These are political parties (currently seven officially registered, of which only the Social Democrats hold an independent position), trade unions, and — for some reason — the Youth Union (the successor to the Soviet Komsomol). Also, legislative assemblies of the three regions and the capital have this right. Irgashev’s only chance was nomination by the SDPT, but they announced a boycott of the elections.

Another curious fact was the refusal to register the chairman of the Democratic Party — the oldest party in Tajikistan besides the Communists. Saidjafar Usmonzoda had been in good standing with Rahmon before these elections: he was one of those who subordinated the party to the government. In the previous presidential elections, Usmonzoda was a candidate and deserves credit for his political experience. Most likely, he collected the required number of signatures, but was not registered for the most banal reason: alphabetic order on the ballot. His surname would have been last, and Rahmon could not allow that — this is his privilege. Ironically, Saidjafar Usmonzoda had the imprudence to officially correct the incorrect (Soviet-era) spelling of his surname, changing it from Ismonov to Usmonzoda. Thus, in previous elections, his “I” was in the middle of the list, but now “U” placed him behind Rahmon — and that was unacceptable, no matter how many signatures he collected.

As for society’s possible reaction to these “elections,” I do not expect protests now. The Armenian, Belarusian, or even Kyrgyz model of events is inapplicable to the realities of Tajikistan. Elections here cannot be a trigger for public unrest: they are organized so that candidates who could theoretically rally people are eliminated beforehand. In Tajikistan, the reason for protests will more likely come in the “Tunisian model”: when some egregious case resulting from anti-people socio-economic policies outrages the broadest layers of the population. The impossibility of a lawful change of power will sooner or later lead to its violent overthrow — and I believe that day is not far off.
